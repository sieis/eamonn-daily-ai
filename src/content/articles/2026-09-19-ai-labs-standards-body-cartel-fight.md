keyDetails:
  - "Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google DeepMind have been meeting since July 2026 to discuss forming an industry-funded standards body modeled on FINRA — the group that oversees U.S. stockbrokers — to set common testing protocols and evaluate frontier models for safety before broad release"
  - "No formal charter, funding commitment, or governance structure exists yet; the three companies reportedly disagree on how much government involvement the body should have, with Anthropic leaning toward direct government partnership and OpenAI favoring voluntary standards plus targeted state-level legislation"
  - "Cohere CEO Aidan Gomez published an essay on September 13 calling the proposal 'a cartel by any other name,' drawing comparisons to the SEC's 1975 credit-rating-agency designation and a 1985 EU auto-parts exemption — arguing both let dominant incumbents write rules that locked out smaller competitors"
  - "Gomez says he supports tougher safeguards, independent testing, and more transparency in principle, but argues the rules should come from a broader set of stakeholders rather than Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google deciding among themselves what 'safe' means for the whole industry"
  - "The fight lands the same week Anthropic separately disclosed that Claude now leads a quarter of the R&D work building its own successor, adding public pressure to the question of whether frontier AI safety should be policed by the biggest labs themselves or by outside rule-makers"
whyItMatters: "Think of this as an argument over who gets to write the rulebook for AI, the same way credit rating agencies or securities regulators write rules that shape an entire industry from the top. A smaller competitor is warning that whatever they agree on will conveniently favor the companies already winning. Either way, the outcome will eventually shape what safety guarantees you get from the AI tools you rely on, and who's allowed to build the next one."
