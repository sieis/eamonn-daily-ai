  - "Only 51% say they have access to the learning and development they need, down from 59% last year"
  - "PwC sorts workers into four groups: 'front-runners' (14%) with strong AI skills, and a 56% 'engine room' that delivers reliably but feels unsupported on AI"

whyItMatters: "Most employees are using AI now, but few are getting real help learning it. A handful of people are pulling far ahead while everyone else feels stuck, and trust in managers is slipping. For a business, that means the gains from AI are likely to be uneven unless training catches up."
consultingAngle: "Two practical moves. First, make training specific and short: pick two or three workflows (customer replies, meeting notes, reporting) and show every employee how to do them with AI, rather than handing out tool licenses and hoping. Second, don't lose your early adopters: the staff who already experiment with AI are the ones most likely to leave, so give them a formal role, such as AI champion, with time and recognition to teach others. A quarterly 'what saved you time this month' session is a cheap way to start."
